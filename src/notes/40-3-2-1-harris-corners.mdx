## The problem

Stereo gives depth from disparity, $Z = fB/d$, so everything depends on matching each point in the left image to the right point in the right image. Matching single pixels by brightness fails: on a smooth surface many shifts look equally good (Fig. 40.7 in the book).

<Goal>
Before matching, decide **which points can be matched without ambiguity**. A point is matchable only if no nearby position looks like it.
</Goal>

Harris works on **one image**. It does not compare left and right. Corners are found in each image separately, then matched (SIFT, 40.3.2.2), then depth is filled in between them (40.3.2.3).

## The idea

A good point looks different when you move it in **any** direction.

- Flat area: moving any way changes nothing.
- Edge: moving across it changes a lot, moving along it changes nothing.
- Corner: every move changes the patch.

So we need a number that says how much a patch changes when it moves. That number is $E$.

## What ℓ is

$\ell$ is the image, written as a function. You give it a position, it gives back a brightness.

- $(x, y)$ is a position (column, row).
- $\ell$ is the whole image.
- $\ell(x, y)$ is one number: the brightness at that position.

The book writes $\ell$ for luminance. Other books write $I(x, y)$ for the same thing.

## Eq. 40.2: the definition of E

<KeyEq name="Eq. 40.2 · sum of squared differences (Harris: auto-correlation function)">
$$
E(\Delta x, \Delta y) = \sum_{(x,y)\in P} \big(\ell(x,y) - \ell(x+\Delta x,\ y+\Delta y)\big)^2
$$
</KeyEq>

$E$ is not derived from anything. It is a **definition**: a measure someone chose for "how much does the window change when I slide it". Each piece has a job.

- $P$ is the small window around the point (for example 5×5 pixels).
- $(\Delta x, \Delta y)$ is a small shift of the window, inside the same image.
- $\ell(x,y)$ is the brightness **before**: the pixel in the original window.
- $\ell(x+\Delta x, y+\Delta y)$ is the brightness **after**: the pixel you land on after moving.
- The difference, before minus after, is how much that one pixel changed.
- **Square** it so changes cannot cancel: $(+50) + (-50) = 0$ would look like "no change", but $50^2 + 50^2 = 5000$ does not.
- **Sum** over the window so one noisy pixel does not decide alone.

Read it like this: $E \approx 0$ means the neighbour position looks the same (bad for matching). $E$ large means it looks different (good).

The largest $E$ cannot tell an edge from a corner (both have a big value somewhere). The **smallest** $E$ over all directions can. So the rule we want is

$$
\text{corner} \iff \min_{\|\mathbf d\| = 1} E(\mathbf d) \text{ is large.}
$$

Trying every angle is slow. The rest of the derivation finds this minimum in closed form.

## Step 1: Taylor expansion of the after term

<Goal>
Get rid of the shifted image $\ell(x+\Delta x, y+\Delta y)$, so $E$ depends on the shift directly.
</Goal>

### What ∂ℓ/∂x · Δx means

$\partial \ell / \partial x$ is a **rate**: brightness change per 1 pixel moved right. $\Delta x$ is a **distance**: how many pixels you move. Rate times distance is the change, the same as speed × time = distance travelled.

<Figure caption="Brightness along one row. Slope 10 per pixel, move 3 pixels: brightness rises 10 × 3 = 30. The run is a position step; the rise is a brightness change.">
  <SlopeFigure />
</Figure>

Be careful with the vertical axis here: it is **brightness**, not the image's $y$ direction. Think of the image as a landscape where bright pixels are high ground.

### Where the formula comes from

Start from the definition of the derivative:

$$
\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial x} = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{\ell(x+\Delta x) - \ell(x)}{\Delta x}
$$

For a small but real $\Delta x$, multiply both sides by $\Delta x$ and move $\ell(x)$ across:

$$
\ell(x+\Delta x) \approx \ell(x) + \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial x}\,\Delta x
$$

In 2D, move in $x$ and then in $y$; each direction adds its own change:

<KeyEq name="First-order Taylor expansion (linear approximation)">
$$
\ell(x+\Delta x,\ y+\Delta y) \approx \ell(x,y) + \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial x}\,\Delta x + \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial y}\,\Delta y
$$
</KeyEq>

Every term on the right is a **brightness**, not a position: brightness before, plus the change from moving in $x$, plus the change from moving in $y$. The slopes convert a position step into a brightness change.

It is "≈" because the full expansion also has $\Delta x^2$, $\Delta x \Delta y$, $\Delta y^2$ terms. For a small shift those are much smaller ($0.1^2 = 0.01$), so they are dropped.

## Step 2: put it back into E

Write $I_x = \partial \ell / \partial x$ and $I_y = \partial \ell / \partial y$ (computed with a Sobel filter). Before minus after:

$$
\ell(x,y) - \big(\ell(x,y) + I_x\Delta x + I_y\Delta y\big) = -\big(I_x\Delta x + I_y\Delta y\big)
$$

The two $\ell(x,y)$ cancel. E only cares about the **change**, and the change depends only on slope × shift. The square that was already in $E$ removes the minus sign:

$$
E(\Delta x, \Delta y) \approx \sum_{P} \big(I_x\,\Delta x + I_y\,\Delta y\big)^2
$$

Expand the square with $(a+b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2$:

$$
\big(I_x\Delta x + I_y\Delta y\big)^2 = I_x^2\,\Delta x^2 + 2\,I_xI_y\,\Delta x\Delta y + I_y^2\,\Delta y^2
$$

$\Delta x$ and $\Delta y$ are the same for every pixel, so they come out of the sum:

$$
E \approx \underbrace{\Big(\sum_P I_x^2\Big)}_{A}\Delta x^2 + 2\underbrace{\Big(\sum_P I_xI_y\Big)}_{B}\Delta x\Delta y + \underbrace{\Big(\sum_P I_y^2\Big)}_{C}\Delta y^2
$$

## Step 3: matrix form

<Goal>
Pack the whole patch into one small object that gives $E$ for any direction.
</Goal>

<KeyEq name="Structure tensor M (second-moment matrix, auto-correlation matrix)">
$$
E(\mathbf d) \approx \mathbf d^\top M\,\mathbf d,\qquad
M = \sum_{P} \begin{bmatrix} I_x^2 & I_xI_y \\ I_xI_y & I_y^2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} A & B \\ B & C \end{bmatrix},\qquad
\mathbf d = \begin{bmatrix}\Delta x \\ \Delta y\end{bmatrix}
$$
</KeyEq>

Check: $[\Delta x\ \Delta y]\,M\,[\Delta x\ \Delta y]^\top = A\Delta x^2 + 2B\Delta x\Delta y + C\Delta y^2$. Three numbers now describe how the patch reacts to every small move. $M$ is symmetric and positive semi-definite, since $\mathbf d^\top M \mathbf d = \sum (\nabla\ell^\top \mathbf d)^2 \ge 0$.

## Step 4: eigenvalues give the weakest direction

For unit $\mathbf d$, the smallest value of $\mathbf d^\top M \mathbf d$ is the smaller eigenvalue of $M$, reached along its eigenvector (Rayleigh quotient). So the rule becomes

<KeyEq name="Corner test (the book's rule; strictly the Shi–Tomasi version)">
$$
\text{corner} \iff \lambda_{\min}(M) > \lambda_c
$$
</KeyEq>

For a 2×2 matrix the eigenvalues have a closed form:

$$
\lambda_{\max,\min} = \frac{A+C}{2} \pm \sqrt{\Big(\frac{A-C}{2}\Big)^2 + B^2}
$$

### Why the Hessian keeps coming up

The Hessian is the matrix of **second derivatives**. It describes curvature, how a function bends. Take second derivatives of $E \approx \sum (I_x\Delta x + I_y\Delta y)^2$ with respect to the shift:

$$
\frac{\partial^2 E}{\partial \Delta x^2} = 2\sum I_x^2,\qquad
\frac{\partial^2 E}{\partial \Delta x\,\partial \Delta y} = 2\sum I_xI_y,\qquad
\frac{\partial^2 E}{\partial \Delta y^2} = 2\sum I_y^2
$$

So the **Hessian of E is 2M**. The eigenvalues of $M$ are the curvature of $E$ along its main directions: a bowl (corner), a valley (edge), or a flat plane (flat).

Do not mix this up with the **Hessian of the image** $\ell$, which uses $\ell_{xx}, \ell_{xy}, \ell_{yy}$. That one is used by the Hessian blob detector, and by SIFT to reject keypoints on edges.

## Step 5: Harris' own score

Harris and Stephens (1988) avoided the square root by using two invariants of $M$: $\det M = AC - B^2 = \lambda_1\lambda_2$ and $\operatorname{tr} M = A + C = \lambda_1 + \lambda_2$.

<KeyEq name="Harris response, k ≈ 0.04–0.06">
$$
R = \det M - k\,(\operatorname{tr} M)^2
$$
</KeyEq>

- Flat: $|R|$ small.
- Edge: $R \approx -k\lambda_{\max}^2$, negative.
- Corner: $R = \lambda^2(1-4k)$ when $\lambda_1 = \lambda_2 = \lambda$, large and positive.

## The algorithm

1. Compute $I_x$, $I_y$ with Sobel filters.
2. Compute $I_x^2$, $I_xI_y$, $I_y^2$ at every pixel.
3. Sum them over the window (usually a Gaussian blur) to get $A$, $B$, $C$.
4. Score each pixel with $\lambda_{\min}$ or $R$.
5. Threshold, then keep only local maxima (non-maximum suppression).

Properties: invariant to rotation and to a brightness offset; the threshold depends on contrast; **not** invariant to scale, which is why SIFT largely replaced it.

<SayIt>
E is the sum of squared differences between the patch and its shifted copy. With a first-order Taylor expansion it becomes a quadratic form $\mathbf d^\top M \mathbf d$ with the structure tensor $M$, which is half the Hessian of $E$. The eigenvalues of $M$ are the change in the strongest and weakest directions, so both large means a corner.
</SayIt>

## Questions from the seminar (2026-10-14)

<QAList>
<QA q="Why is E equal to that? Where does it come from?">
It is a definition, not a result. It was chosen to measure how much the window changes when you slide it: compare each pixel before and after, square so signs cannot cancel, add up over the window. It is the standard sum of squared differences (SSD).
</QA>
<QA q="Is ℓ a point?">
No. $\ell$ is the whole image. $(x, y)$ is the point, and $\ell(x, y)$ is the brightness at that point.
</QA>
<QA q="What is ℓ(x+Δx, y+Δy)?">
The brightness of the pixel you land on after moving $\Delta x$ right and $\Delta y$ down. Its approximation is the first-order Taylor expansion $\ell(x,y) + I_x\Delta x + I_y\Delta y$.
</QA>
<QA q="Why does the Taylor expansion look like that?">
It is the definition of the derivative, rearranged. Slope = change ÷ distance, so for a small move, change ≈ slope × distance. New brightness = old brightness + change.
</QA>
<QA q="Why did ℓ(x,y) disappear from E?">
Before minus after: $\ell - (\ell + I_x\Delta x + I_y\Delta y)$. The $\ell(x,y)$ is in both, so it cancels. Only the change is left.
</QA>
<QA q="Why square?">
So positive and negative changes cannot cancel, so big changes count more than small noise, and because a square is smooth. After Taylor, expanding the square produces $I_x^2$, $I_xI_y$, $I_y^2$, which are the entries of $M$.
</QA>
<QA q="What is the Hessian?">
The matrix of second derivatives, which measures curvature. Here the Hessian of $E$ with respect to the shift is exactly $2M$.
</QA>
</QAList>
